# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper introduces SciAudit, a large-scale, multidisciplinary benchmark designed to evaluate Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) on open-ended scientific error detection across full research papers. The benchmark spans 16 scientific domains and comprises two complementary subsets: a Gold subset containing 2,938 human-verified real-world errors across 293 problematic papers, and a Silver subset containing 5,441 controlled synthetic errors injected into 527 high-quality papers via a reciprocal multi-model Draft–Check–Refine pipeline. To evaluate intermediate diagnostic quality alongside binary detection, the authors propose a continuous quality-aware evaluation framework across four dimensions (*Where*, *What*, *Why*, and *Which*), and benchmark 13 proprietary and open-source MLLMs, revealing that while current models struggle with autonomous end-to-end discovery, they can accurately verify most missed errors when explicitly prompted with candidate locations.

## Strengths
* **Comprehensive and Scalable Benchmark Design:** SciAudit unifies target-absent, open-ended full-paper error detection with fine-grained process evaluation across 16 scientific domains, scaling human-verified real-world error annotations by over an order of magnitude compared to prior real-world benchmarks while demonstrating strong ranking consistency with its multi-model synthetic Silver subset.
* **Diagnostic Quality-Aware Evaluation Framework:** Rather than relying solely on binary true-positive counts, the proposed evaluation framework jointly scores error localization, evidence coverage and conciseness, ordered reasoning steps, and error categorization, preventing models from receiving full credit for unsubstantiated guesses.
* **Insightful Empirical Diagnosis of Discovery vs. Verification:** By re-evaluating models on the exact locations of errors they missed during open-ended full-paper scanning, the authors provide compelling empirical evidence that current MLLM failures stem primarily from bottlenecks in proactive long-context discovery and composite multi-step reasoning rather than a lack of underlying domain knowledge.

## Weaknesses
* **Omission of Multi-Stage and Agentic Scanning Baselines:** Although a central conclusion of the paper is that MLLMs fail primarily due to limitations in autonomous error discovery across full papers, all experiments evaluate models exclusively under a single-pass, full-document inference paradigm. Please consider evaluating structured section-by-section or multi-agent scanning workflows to clarify how much of the discovery bottleneck is an artifact of single-pass prompting versus intrinsic reasoning limitations (Reference Paper: PaperAudit-Bench: Benchmarking Error Detection in Research Papers for Critical Automated Peer Review, Songjun Tu et al., 2026; Reference Paper: TreeReview: A Dynamic Tree of Questions Framework for Deep and Efficient LLM-based Scientific Peer Review, Yuan Chang et al., 2025; Reference Paper: MARG: Multi-Agent Review Generation for Scientific Papers, Mike D'Arcy et al., 2024; Reference Paper: DocR1: Evidence Page-Guided GRPO for Multi-Page Document Understanding, Junyu Xiong et al., 2026).
* **Mathematical Formulation of Evidence Conciseness Under Many-to-Many Alignment:** In Equation 2, the evidence conciseness term $\frac{m_E(g,p)}{\max(|p.e|, m_E(g,p))}$ uses the number of matched *gold* evidence items $m_E(g,p)$ in the numerator rather than the number of matched *predicted* items. Because many-to-many matching is allowed, a prediction that merges multiple gold items into one can output additional irrelevant or redundant evidence items without penalty as long as $|p.e| \le m_E(g,p)$, while valid splits of a single gold item are unfairly penalized; the authors might consider reformulating the numerator using the count of matched predicted evidence items.
* **Potential Candidate-Mining Bias in Gold and Confounds in Verification Analysis:** In the Gold subset, 86.6% of verified errors originate from candidates mined by three evaluated MLLMs (`GPT-5.4`, `Gemini-3.1-Pro`, and `Qwen-3.6-Plus`) that were also permitted to propose errors unmentioned in the public reviews, and the missed-error verification experiment (Table 4) tests only positive error locations without negative control passages. Please consider adding an ablation isolating review-grounded and expert-added Gold errors, as well as including error-free negative control locations in the verification experiment to rule out affirmative bias.
* **Incomplete Operational Details and Prior Art Comparisons:** Several methodological details—such as how the four capability categories were assigned, how text-only Silver modifications affect multimodal perception error symmetry, and the exact definitions of complexity bins in Figure 5—are not fully specified in the text. Additionally, the related work comparison could be strengthened by discussing prior real-world withdrawn-paper benchmarks alongside clarifyng how the proposed quality-aware metrics build upon existing process-aware and span-level inconsistency metrics (Reference Paper: WithdrarXiv: A Large-Scale Dataset for Retraction Study, Vishisht Rao et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Reviewing Scientific Papers for Critical Problems With Reasoning LLMs: Baseline Approaches and Automatic Evaluation, Tianmai M. Zhang and Neil F. Abernethy, 2025; Reference Paper: Not Search, But Scan: Benchmarking MLLMs on Scan-Oriented Academic Paper Reasoning, Rongjin Li et al., 2026; Reference Paper: On Finding Inconsistencies in Documents, Charles J. Lovering et al., 2025).

### [Quality-Aware Evaluation Framework & Experimental Setup] (Pages: 6, 26-28, 34-36)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Formulation of the Evidence Conciseness Term in $S_2(g, p)$ Under Many-to-Many Alignment (Page 6, Section 3.3, Equation 2; Page 27, Appendix A.6.2, Prompt A.13):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 3.3(ii) (Page 6, lines 290–298), the evidence score $S_2(g, p)$ is designed to *"balance evidence completeness against prediction conciseness by rewarding complete gold-evidence coverage and penalizing redundant predicted evidence."* It defines $m_E(g, p) = \left|\{e_g \in g.e : \exists e_p \in p.e, e_g \sim e_p\}\right|$ as the number of *gold* evidence items covered by the prediction, and specifies:
     $$S_2(g, p) = \sqrt{\frac{m_E(g, p)}{|g.e|} \cdot \frac{m_E(g, p)}{\max(|p.e|, m_E(g, p))}}$$
     However, Prompt A.13 (Page 27, Rule 5, lines 1431–1436) explicitly permits many-to-many alignment where predicted evidence items may either merge or split gold evidence items (*"the Ground Truth may split several verification points into separate evidence items, while the prediction may merge them into one item, or vice versa"*). Because the conciseness factor $\frac{m_E(g, p)}{\max(|p.e|, m_E(g, p))}$ compares $m_E(g, p)$ (measured in units of *matched gold items*) against $|p.e|$ (measured in units of *predicted items*), three issues arise:
     1. *Unpenalized redundant evidence under merging:* When a single predicted evidence item merges and covers multiple gold evidence items such that $m_E(g, p)$ exceeds the number of matched predicted items, the model can simultaneously output completely unmatched, redundant, or hallucinated predicted evidence items without incurring any penalty as long as $|p.e| \le m_E(g, p)$. For example, if a gold error has $|g.e| = 3$ evidence items and a model outputs $|p.e| = 3$ items—where $1$ merged item covers all $3$ gold items ($m_E(g, p) = 3$) and the other $2$ items are completely irrelevant—the conciseness factor evaluates to $\frac{3}{\max(3, 3)} = 1.0$, yielding a perfect score of $S_2(g, p) = 1.0$.
     2. *Penalty on valid splitting:* Conversely, if $|g.e| = 1$ and a prediction validly splits that evidence into two concise, non-redundant predicted items ($|p.e| = 2, m_E(g, p) = 1$), the conciseness factor evaluates to $\frac{1}{\max(2, 1)} = 0.5$, penalizing the prediction as if half of its evidence were redundant even though both predicted items are matched in `matched_prediction_evidence_ids`.
     3. *Division-by-zero boundary condition:* If a matched prediction outputs an empty evidence list ($|p.e| = 0$, which forces $m_E(g, p) = 0$), the denominator $\max(|p.e|, m_E(g, p))$ evaluates to $0$, resulting in an undefined $0/0$ expression unless explicitly guarded.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Because Prompt A.13 already returns `matched_prediction_evidence_ids` for each ground-truth evidence item, the number of matched *predicted* evidence items $m_P(g, p) = \left|\{e_p \in p.e : \exists e_g \in g.e, e_g \sim e_p\}\right|$ can be computed directly as the cardinality of the union of `matched_prediction_evidence_ids`. Replacing $\frac{m_E(g, p)}{\max(|p.e|, m_E(g, p))}$ with $\frac{m_P(g, p)}{|p.e|}$ (and explicitly setting $S_2(g, p) = 0$ whenever $m_E(g, p) = 0$ or $|p.e| = 0$) would ensure that every unmatched predicted evidence item is penalized while valid splits and merges are handled symmetrically.

2. **Unspecified Paper-Level Aggregation Protocol and Zero-Prediction Boundary Conditions (Page 6, Section 3.3, Equation 6):**
   - **Clarity / Reproducibility Concern:** Equation (6) (Page 6, lines 316–319) defines the metrics for an individual paper with ground-truth set $G$ and prediction set $P$ as:
     $$\text{Q-Recall} = \frac{\text{Q-TP}}{|G|}, \quad \text{Q-Precision} = \frac{\text{Q-TP}}{|P|}, \quad \text{Q-F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Q-Precision} \cdot \text{Q-Recall}}{\text{Q-Precision} + \text{Q-Recall}}$$
     Two mathematical specifications needed to reproduce the dataset-wide paper-level results (e.g., in Table 10 on Page 36, as well as the main benchmark tables) are omitted in Section 3.3:
     1. *Boundary handling when $|P| = 0$ or $\text{Q-TP} = 0$:* Models are instructed to output `{"errors":[]}` when no sufficiently supported errors are found, resulting in $|P| = 0$ (and $\text{Q-TP} = 0$). For any paper with $|P| = 0$, $\text{Q-Precision} = 0/0$ is undefined; similarly, whenever $\text{Q-TP} = 0$ (even if $|P| > 0$), $\text{Q-Precision} + \text{Q-Recall} = 0$, making $\text{Q-F1} = 0/0$ undefined. The text does not state whether $\text{Q-Precision}$ and $\text{Q-F1}$ are defined as $0$ in these boundary cases or handled differently during averaging.
     2. *Dataset-level aggregation order:* Section 3.3 does not specify how single-paper metrics in Equation (6) are aggregated across all $N$ papers in a subset. 3. **Sensitivity of Contiguous Longest Common Substring ($\text{LCStr}$) on Location Strings and Unspecified Granularity ($S_1$, Page 6, Equation 1):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 3.3(i) (Page 6, lines 284–289), $S_1(g, p)$ averages the normalized longest common *substring* ($\text{LCStr}$) similarity over `error_text` ($t$) and `error_location` ($l$):
     $$\text{sim}(g.t, p.t) = \frac{|\text{LCStr}(g.t, p.t)|}{\sqrt{|g.t| \cdot |p.t|}}$$
     First, Section 3.3 does not specify whether $|\text{LCStr}(\cdot, \cdot)|$ and $|\cdot|$ are computed at the character level or word/token level, nor what string normalization (such as case folding, whitespace collapsing, or punctuation stripping) is applied beforehand. Second, unlike `error_text` ($t$), which is a verbatim passage from the paper, `error_location` ($l$) is a semi-structured string combining the page number and nearest section or subsection title. Because $\text{LCStr}$ requires a single unbroken contiguous match, applying it directly to `error_location` strings can heavily penalize benign formatting variations that Prompt A.12 (Page 26, Rule 4, line 1375) explicitly deems acceptable. For example, if $g.l$ is `"Page 6, Section 3.3.4 Loading efficiency"` (40 characters) and $p.l$ is `"Page 6, Sec. 3.3.4: Loading efficiency"` (38 characters), the abbreviation and colon split the match so that the longest contiguous common substring is only `" Loading efficiency"` (19 characters), yielding $\text{sim}(g.l, p.l) \approx 0.49$ despite identifying the exact same page, section number, and heading.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Specify the exact granularity (character vs. token) and text normalization rules used prior to computing Equation (1), and either justify contiguous $\text{LCStr}$ or consider whether token-level Longest Common Subsequence ($\text{LCS}$), token overlap, or structured page/section comparison would provide a less brittle measure for $\text{sim}(g.l, p.l)$.

### [Empirical Results, Fine-Grained Analysis & Failure Cases] (Pages: 7-9, 37-46)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Potential Confounding from Affirmative Bias and Missing Negative Controls in Missed Error Verification (Section 4.3, Table 4, Page 9):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In the *Missed Error Analysis* (lines 466–477), the authors gather errors that four models failed to detect during initial open-ended evaluation across 50 sampled papers, provide each model with the location of each missed error, and prompt it to verify whether it is an error. Based on the resulting 52.5%–81.3% verification accuracy in Table 4, the authors conclude in Section 4.3 and Section 5 that "many missed errors are not beyond the models' ability to judge correctly, but are instead overlooked during autonomous detection" (lines 474–476). However, as described, this verification evaluation presents models exclusively with positive instances (locations known to contain genuine errors) and omits negative control instances (error-free passages from the same papers). In the immediately preceding *False Positives Analysis* (lines 460–465), the authors show that the evaluated models already exhibit "a tendency toward being overly strict" and frequently misclassify normal academic expressions, ordinary study limitations, or minor wording issues as scientific errors. When an instruction-tuned MLLM is explicitly pointed to a specific location and asked whether it contains an error, sycophancy or affirmative bias can cause it to agree and manufacture or overstate a flaw at a high rate. While Table 4 also reports high reasoning quality scores (*Which*, *What*, and *Why* ranging from 67.6% to 86.0%), which partially mitigates the concern that models are guessing blindly, it is not specified whether those dimension scores are computed only over the subset of cases verified as true positives or across all tested instances, nor whether "Accuracy" is a simple binary (`Yes`/`No`) classification rate or requires passing the semantic error-matching criteria (via Prompt A.12). Furthermore, unlike all other experimental tasks (whose prompts are documented in Appendix A), the exact prompt and input granularity for this verification task (specifically, whether "location" provides only the section/page string `error_location` or also the exact target sentence `error_text`) are omitted from the manuscript.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Include a matched set of negative control locations (randomly sampled error-free passages from the same 50 papers) in the verification experiment and report false-positive rates, specificity, or balanced accuracy alongside positive verification recall. Additionally, clarify in Section 4.3 how `Accuracy` and the *Which*, *What*, and *Why* scores in Table 4 are defined and conditioned, and document the exact verification prompt and input schema in Appendix A.

2. **Omission of Multi-Stage or Agentic Scanning Baselines (Pages 1–3, Abstract, Section 1, and Section 2; Sections 4.1–4.2, Pages 7–8; Section 5, Page 9):**
   - **Potential Concern:** All 13 MLLMs in Section 4 are evaluated exclusively under a single-pass, full-paper inference paradigm, where the entire parsed paper is fed into the context window at once and the model outputs all detected errors in a single completion (capped at 16,384 tokens). While this establishes baseline capabilities for standalone models, a central diagnostic finding highlighted in the Abstract (lines 027–030), Section 1 (lines 089–094), Section 4.3, and Section 5 is that current MLLMs fail in open-ended scientific error detection primarily due to "limitations in error discovery" across full papers rather than insufficient domain knowledge (lines 474–485; evidenced by models verifying `52.5%–81.3%` of missed errors when given explicit candidate locations). Prior works cited in Section 1 and Section 2—such as `PaperAudit-Bench` (Tu et al., 2026), which introduces `PaperAudit-Review` (comparing fast single-pass inference against section-aware scanning conditioned on document-level global memory and deeper plan-driven multi-agent verification), as well as structured reviewing frameworks like `TreeReview` (Chang et al., 2025) and `ReviewGrounder` (Li et al., 2026b)—have already demonstrated that single-pass global scanning over long manuscripts suffers from low recall on sparse, distributed errors. Evaluating only single-pass full-document prompting leaves open the question of whether the $<40\%$ Q-Recall ceiling reported in Table 2 is an inherent reasoning limitation of current MLLMs on `SciAudit` or largely an artifact of single-pass full-paper generation, and whether existing structured or section-by-section agentic scanning workflows already mitigate this discovery gap.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section 1 that the baseline evaluation measures single-pass end-to-end MLLM inference, and evaluate at least one representative model (e.g., `GPT-5.4` or `Qwen3.5-27B`) under a section-by-section scanning or coarse-to-fine multi-stage workflow (such as the section-aware memory workflow from Tu et al., 2026, or a two-stage propose-then-verify pipeline) on the 50-paper diagnostic subset used in Section 4.3 to quantify how much of the discovery gap between end-to-end recall (Table 2) and localized verification (Table 4) is bridged by structured decomposition.

3. **Draft-Difficulty Confounding and Localized Self-Generator Effects in Cross-Evaluation (Section 4.2, Page 8; Appendix C.2, Table 11, Page 37):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 4.2 (lines 425–426) and Appendix C.2 (lines 1969–1978), the authors cite Table 11 to conclude that model rankings remain stable across Silver subsets generated by different Draft models and that there is "no systematic self-generator advantage: several detectors perform better on drafts generated by other models than on their own." However, directly comparing a detector's raw scores horizontally across columns is confounded by large baseline differences in the intrinsic detectability of errors produced by different Draft models:
     1. *Column-wise difficulty confounds:* Across all six detectors, errors drafted by `Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro` (detector Q-F1 ranging from 26.8% to 54.2%, mean 37.1%) and `Gemini-3.1-Pro` (23.4% to 52.9%, mean 35.9%) are substantially easier to detect than errors drafted by `Claude-Sonnet-4.6` (19.1% to 42.3%, mean 27.3%) or `GPT-5.4` (19.6% to 48.4%, mean 30.8%). Because `Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro` and `Gemini-3.1-Pro` generate systematically easier errors overall, almost all detectors achieve their highest absolute scores on those two columns rather than on their own column.
     2. *Relative within-column self-preference:* When controlling for column difficulty by examining relative detector rankings and score gaps within each Draft column, localized generator–detector affinities emerge:
        - On the five subsets drafted by other models, `Grok-4.3` consistently ranks last or tied for last (scoring 19.1%–26.8% Q-F1 and trailing `GPT-5.4` by 22.6–29.5 Q-F1 points). However, on the subset drafted by `Grok-4.3` itself, `Grok-4.3` jumps to 4th place (`29.1%` Q-F1 and `19.5%` Q-Recall)—surpassing `Claude-Sonnet-4.6` (`28.9%` Q-F1) and cutting its gap to `GPT-5.4` nearly in half (to 13.4 Q-F1 points, reaching 68.5% of `GPT-5.4`'s Q-F1 compared to 40.5%–50.4% on all other columns), while `GPT-5.4` drops to its lowest Q-Precision across all six subsets (`53.0%`).
        - `GPT-5.4`'s lead over the second-best detector (`Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro`) is largest on `GPT-5.4`'s own generated drafts (+15.7 Q-F1 points: `48.4%` vs. `32.7%`; +15.8 Q-Recall points: `37.5%` vs. `21.7%`), whereas on `Grok-4.3`'s drafts `GPT-5.4` leads `Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro` by only +1.4 Q-F1 points (`42.5%` vs. `41.1%`).
        - Similarly, `Gemini-3.1-Pro` outperforms `Qwen-3.6-Plus` in Q-F1 on `GPT-5.4` drafts (`29.9%` vs. `29.6%`), whereas `Qwen-3.6-Plus` outperforms `Gemini-3.1-Pro` on all other five draft subsets.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Revise the discussion of Table 11 in Appendix C.2 (and Section 4.2) to account for column-wise draft difficulty (e.g., by analyzing column-normalized relative performance or row- and column-centered residuals rather than relying solely on raw row-wise comparisons), acknowledge the localized self-detection boost observed for `Grok-4.3` and `GPT-5.4`, and briefly discuss why certain generators (`Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro` and `Gemini-3.1-Pro`) produce more easily detectable errors despite the reciprocal Check–Refine pipeline.
